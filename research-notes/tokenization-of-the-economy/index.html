Research Note · Technology, Growth, and Civilization

The Tokenization of the Economy: AI, Specialization, and the Retreat of Economic Boundaries

Date
August 15, 2026
Author
Arthur Palmer

Since Adam Smith, specialization has been one of the central organizing principles of modern economic life. The division of labor allowed individuals to master narrower tasks, firms to coordinate increasingly complex production processes, and societies to accumulate forms of expertise that no single person could command. Industrialization deepened this logic. Economic progress came not only from better machines, but from decomposing production into finer activities and assigning those activities to specialized workers, occupations, departments, firms, professions, and institutions.

There was a deep reason this architecture worked. Human cognition is bounded. Memory is limited, learning takes time, and mastery in one field normally comes at the expense of mastery elsewhere. A surgeon cannot simultaneously become an electrical engineer, tax attorney, software architect, and molecular biologist. A firm therefore builds specialized departments; universities divide knowledge into disciplines; governments construct specialized agencies; countries develop comparative advantages around capabilities they have accumulated over time.

Specialization can therefore be understood as a divide-and-conquer response to cognitive scarcity. Society partitions a problem too large for any individual to solve and then develops mechanisms for recombining the specialized pieces. Markets coordinate some through prices. Firms coordinate others through hierarchy. Professions create credentials and standards. Universities reproduce specialized knowledge. Capital markets allocate resources among relatively distinct sectors and business models.

Artificial intelligence may be changing the constraint that gave this architecture much of its value.

The important development is not simply that AI can automate individual tasks. Increasingly, heterogeneous economic activities can be translated into forms machines can interpret, process, compare, verify, route, and recombine. Text, images, code, data, equations, precedents, designs, instructions, simulations, and intermediate results can all enter the same computational environment. Problems that historically belonged to separate groups of specialists can increasingly be decomposed into machine-legible components and recombined without the user personally traversing the full knowledge path associated with each field.

Call this process economic tokenization.

The term is not being used in the cryptocurrency sense. A token is any machine-legible representation that allows some component of economic activity to be detached from the particular specialist, department, firm, or institution that historically performed it and passed through a common computational system. Once an activity can be sufficiently represented, transmitted, evaluated, and recombined in this way, the activity becomes more contestable.

That distinction matters. Tokenization does not imply that every human performing a tokenizable activity disappears. It means that the boundary protecting that activity from alternative production arrangements becomes weaker.

The transformation is therefore not merely one of productivity. It is a change in the partition and topology of economic activity.

Specialization as an Endogenous Partition

Most economic analysis begins with tasks or sectors already given. But many of the categories we treat as fixed are themselves products of earlier technological and cognitive constraints.

A job is a bundle of activities. A department is another bundle. A profession, firm, research discipline, and even part of a national comparative advantage can also be understood as a historically stable grouping of capabilities. Those groupings persist because the cost of crossing their boundaries was high enough to make specialization efficient.

The old economy therefore did not merely assign people to tasks. It created a partition of economic activity.

Let the underlying activity space be divided into blocks:

P0 = {B1, B2, …, BK}.

The blocks become occupations, departments, professions, firms, industries, or disciplines because performing and coordinating activities across blocks is expensive. Human cognition, training time, organizational communication, credentialing, contracting, language, geography, and measurement all contribute to those costs.

This point is not new. Barzel emphasized the role of measurement costs in defining transactions, and Baldwin later described production as a task network whose transaction boundaries tend to occur at “thin crossing points” where transfers can be defined, counted, and valued relatively cheaply. The modularity and organizational-design literatures likewise treat boundaries as economic objects rather than natural facts. The relevant AI question is therefore not whether boundaries move when coordination or measurement costs change. They do.

The distinctive possibility created by AI is that a common representational technology can thin many heterogeneous crossing points at once.

A business manager can now call on legal analysis, software development, market research, statistical work, writing, design, financial modeling, and technical explanation through a common machine interface. A researcher can reach methods and literatures outside the field in which she was trained. A small enterprise can access capabilities that previously required multiple departments.

The relevant cost reduction is therefore not simply a decline in the cost of performing activity i. It is a decline in the cost of composing capabilities that previously sat on opposite sides of an economic boundary.

AI acts as a solvent not on specialization itself, but on the organizational boundaries historically required to access specialization.

Specialization Does Not Disappear. It Moves Down a Layer.

This distinction resolves an apparent paradox.

Falling coordination costs can make finer specialization more efficient. Becker and Murphy showed long ago that lower coordination costs can support greater specialization rather than less. AI itself may be an extreme example. Behind a simple interface may sit highly specialized models, tools, databases, agents, retrieval systems, verification routines, and computational infrastructure. Machine production can become more specialized even as the human-facing organization becomes more generalist.

The result is not:

specialization → generalization.

It is closer to:

human specialization → machine specialization + human integration.

Specialization moves down a layer while integration moves up a layer.

A twentieth-century enterprise often needed separate groups for finance, marketing, software, legal work, research, procurement, and operations. A future enterprise may retain access to all of these capabilities while employing far fewer dedicated human specialists at the point of use. The machine system supplies increasingly specialized depth; a smaller set of humans decides which capabilities to invoke, how to combine them, and what to do with the result.

The generalist therefore returns in a new form.

The traditional generalist lacked the specialist's depth. The AI-enabled generalist can increasingly access depth without personally storing all of it.

The human comparative advantage shifts toward problem selection, integration, judgment, and action. But this raises an immediate objection: who verifies the machine-produced output?

Verification Is Not the Final Wall

One tempting answer is that AI substitutes for specialized production but not for verification. That would leave specialists as residual checkers. In some domains this will happen, especially during transition. But it cannot be the general theory.

Verification is itself an economic activity.

If a junior analyst's work can be tokenized, so can much of the manager's review. The same is true of the specialist checker, risk review, legal review, and parts of the committee process.

A human organization that historically looked like

junior → manager → specialist → risk/legal → committee

can increasingly be approximated by

AIG → AIV1 → AIV2 → AIA → AIC,

where one system generates, others verify, another is instructed to attack the result adversarially, and another reconciles disagreements.

This can dramatically reduce the cost of multilayer checking. A small company may eventually be able to run a review architecture that historically existed only inside a large bank, hospital, law firm, engineering company, or government agency.

But cheap checking is not the same as credible assurance.

If the generator and verifier rely on the same data, assumptions, model family, or representation of the problem, their errors may be strongly correlated. Ten systems repeating the same hidden mistake do not create ten independent checks.

The relevant scarce object is therefore not simply verification. It is independent assurance.

This distinction matters because assurance contains at least two different elements.

The first is epistemic independence: the checker must be capable of generating errors that are sufficiently different from those of the producer. Different data, models, methods, measurements, priors, or physical observations can create this form of independence.

The second is economic exposure: the checker, certifier, insurer, auditor, or approving institution may have a distinct payoff, liability, reputation, or capital position that is affected if the decision is wrong.

These are not the same thing. Two auditors can be legally separate while relying on the same defective analytical system. Conversely, two measurement systems can supply genuinely independent information even if neither bears financial responsibility for the final action.

A credible assurance architecture can require both.

Recursive Tokenization

This leads to a more dynamic view of the transition.

AI does not face one permanent verification wall. It repeatedly tokenizes the activity that has become the new bottleneck.

At one stage, production is scarce.

AI tokenizes production.

Verification then becomes relatively scarce.

AI tokenizes verification.

Correlated verification becomes cheap and abundant.

The relative value of low-correlation information, independent measurement, distinct incentives, and credible exposure rises inside the assurance bundle.

The process is recursive:

B0 AI⟶ B1 AI⟶ B2 AI⟶ …

where Bk is the current boundary between activity that can be represented and resolved inside the machine system and activity that still requires a harder-to-tokenize complement.

This is closer to the true meaning of AI as a solvent. The solvent does not dissolve every boundary at once. It keeps eating into the architecture, exposing a deeper residual constraint.

This does not mean the absolute value of independent checking must rise forever. Better models may make fewer errors even if those errors are more correlated. The more robust prediction is relative: as ordinary checking becomes cheap, the marginal value of another highly correlated check collapses relative to the value of a genuinely independent source of information or exposure.

Recursive tokenization therefore changes the composition of assurance even when the total cost of assurance eventually falls.

Grounding, Authority, and Consequence

At some point, assurance cannot consist only of manipulating representations of other representations. The system has to reconnect to the world.

An AI can verify the algebra of an acquisition model. Another can reconstruct the model independently. A third can compare assumptions with historical data. A fourth can search for contradictory evidence. Yet whether the acquisition should actually proceed may still depend on facts that require a distinct physical or institutional channel: whether the factory operates as represented, whether management is trustworthy, whether customers behave as assumed, whether a regulator will intervene, whether a physical system is safe, or whether an institution will honor an obligation.

This is a grounding problem.

Grounding should not be romanticized as permanently human. Sensors, measurement systems, audits, remote monitoring, robotics, and machine observation can all reduce grounding costs. But the economic point survives: information that comes from the same representational pipeline is not a substitute for every form of independent contact with the underlying state of the world.

Authority is different. It is not a technological residual. It is an institutional assignment.

A machine can recommend a course of action, but many systems require somebody or some recognized institution to have the legal or organizational right to act. A court ruling, medical authorization, regulatory approval, capital allocation, corporate acquisition, and military decision are not merely analytical outputs. They are exercises of institutional authority.

AI can therefore create a large gap between technical capability and institutional admissibility. Competence can become cheap without immediately changing who is permitted to exercise it.

Consequence-bearing adds another layer. Someone ultimately absorbs the loss if the decision is wrong.

This is where balance sheets matter.

A small operating firm may be able to rent AI capability from one provider and assurance from another, but consequential activity often still requires a party with capital, insurance, certification, legal standing, reputation, or regulatory approval. The ability to perform a task and the ability to credibly stand behind it are different economic capabilities.

That distinction becomes increasingly important as tokenization advances.

The Compression of Economic Distance

Seen this way, tokenization and economic topology are naturally connected.

Economic distance is not merely geographic. Two people can sit in the same building and be far apart economically because their knowledge, credentials, departments, languages, incentives, or institutional roles are difficult to bridge. Conversely, two people thousands of miles apart can be economically adjacent if capability can be transmitted cheaply.

Tokenization compresses several forms of distance at once.

It compresses knowledge distance because specialist capability becomes easier to access. It compresses organizational distance because capabilities once requiring separate departments can be called through a common machine layer. It compresses geographic distance because much cognitive production can be delivered electronically. It compresses institutional distance when services historically bundled inside one organization can be separated and obtained elsewhere.

The compression is highly uneven.

Land does not disappear. Electricity must still be generated somewhere. Physical surgery still requires interaction with a patient. Manufacturing requires materials and equipment. Trust, legal authority, ownership, political legitimacy, and many forms of physical execution remain located.

The result is therefore not the end of geography. It is a redistribution of which kinds of geography matter.

If cognitive capability becomes increasingly portable, the relative value of non-tokenizable bottlenecks rises: energy, land, infrastructure, physical access, trusted relationships, institutional legitimacy, scarce natural resources, and the right to make binding decisions.

The geography of knowledge may flatten while the geography of physical and institutional scarcity becomes more important.

From Replaceability to Contestability

It is tempting to say that anything that can be tokenized can be replaced. A better proposition is that anything that can be sufficiently tokenized becomes contestable.

A worker becomes more contestable when the capability attached to the job can be obtained through another arrangement.

A department becomes more contestable when a small number of employees using AI can perform functions that once required a specialized staff.

A firm becomes more contestable when capability accumulated inside its organizational boundary becomes available externally.

An institution becomes more contestable when the analytical or informational services it historically bundled together can be accessed without passing through the institution.

Even a country becomes more contestable when a national comparative advantage rests on capabilities whose location ceases to matter.

This has two opposing implications for small countries.

A country specializing in routine business services, software outsourcing, translation, financial processing, research assistance, or other portable cognitive work may discover that its existing comparative advantage is being commoditized.

But a small country with strong institutions, capital, energy, physical infrastructure, and capable decision makers may also gain access to a cognitive capability set that once required a far larger domestic population.

Tokenization may therefore reduce the minimum efficient scale of some operating organizations and some national capabilities.

Yet consequence-bearing can move the other way. If credible assurance requires diversified balance sheets, regulatory standing, insurance capacity, or large pools of capital, then the minimum efficient scale of assurance may rise even as the minimum efficient scale of production falls.

This produces a more precise version of the post-tokenization barbell:

small decentralized operating nodes + large concentrated infrastructure and assurance nodes.

Capital Markets and the Mispricing of the Transition

This transition creates a distinctive capital-market problem.

Traditional industries are organized vertically: software, healthcare, advertising, education, legal services, financial services, consulting. Yet the same horizontal AI capability can increasingly substitute into all of them.

Every vertical market can therefore be transformed without every AI application firm capturing the economic value of that transformation.

The apparent addressable markets are not additive.

Capital markets may initially value each vertical manifestation as though it represents an independent source of durable rent, even when many are simply different interfaces to the same increasingly commoditized underlying capability.

At the same time, the transition creates genuine scarcity upstream. Compute, energy, semiconductor capacity, data-center infrastructure, high-quality data, distribution, physical assets, assurance capacity, and institutional permissions can all become bottlenecks.

This can produce a striking combination:

cognitive abundance downstream + scarcity rents upstream.

The resulting investment boom need not be based on false beliefs about AI. AI can be technologically transformative and still produce poor returns for many investors.

Indeed, technological success may be what destroys some of the scarcity rents on which current valuations are based.

The central capital-market distinction is therefore between value creation and rent capture.

Tokenization may create enormous social value by making capabilities cheap and widely available precisely because it destroys the pricing power of many firms that provide those capabilities.

The Old Economy Meets a Fluid System

The deepest friction may come from institutions built around stable specialization.

Labor markets classify people by occupations. Universities divide knowledge into disciplines. Firms build career ladders within functional specialties. Professional licensing attaches capability to certified individuals. Regulators assign responsibility through organizational boundaries. Capital markets classify businesses into industries whose boundaries are assumed to be economically meaningful.

Tokenization changes capability faster than these institutions can reorganize.

The resulting transition may therefore appear contradictory.

Firms can lay off specialists while complaining about shortages of people capable of exercising judgment.

Productivity can rise while entry-level hiring collapses.

AI application prices can fall while capital expenditure on AI infrastructure rises.

The supply of machine-generated analysis can explode while low-correlation information, legal standing, insurance capacity, or trusted authorization becomes more valuable.

A small company can acquire capabilities that once required a large organization while becoming more dependent on a small number of upstream model, infrastructure, insurance, certification, and capital providers.

These are not separate anomalies. They are signatures of a system in which the old partition is dissolving before a new one has stabilized.

The Post-Tokenization Economy

The long-run result is unlikely to be an economy without specialization.

Specialization will remain deeply embedded in models, tools, datasets, scientific knowledge, physical infrastructure, and specialized machine systems. Indeed, specialization inside the machine layer may become more extreme.

What changes is where specialization resides and how economic actors access it.

The old economy placed much specialized capability inside human beings and then organized those people into occupations, firms, professions, institutions, and countries so the capabilities could be recombined.

The emerging economy increasingly places specialized capability inside a machine-accessible layer and allows humans and organizations to reach across old boundaries through more general interfaces.

The partition of economic activity therefore changes.

Previously distant capabilities become adjacent. Some intermediaries disappear. Some firms become smaller. Some institutions lose their monopoly over expertise. Some countries lose comparative advantage while others gain disproportionate leverage from physical and institutional assets. Verification itself becomes cheaper and more automated, shifting the composition of assurance toward independent information, separate exposure, credible capital, and institutional standing.

The central economic question of the AI era may therefore not be how many jobs machines can automate.

It may be how rapidly society can reorganize an economic system whose underlying boundaries are recursively retreating.

Since Adam Smith, economic organization has largely been built around the productivity gains from specialization under bounded human cognition. AI tokenization changes that constraint. By converting increasingly heterogeneous activities into machine-legible and recombinable units, it changes the optimal partition of economic activity, compresses the distance between capabilities, and makes workers, firms, institutions, and even countries more contestable. As production becomes tokenized, verification can be tokenized as well. What remains scarce is progressively less the ability to produce or repeat analysis and increasingly the ability to supply independent information, credible exposure, physical grounding, capital, and lawful authority.

The post-tokenization economy will therefore not simply produce the same things with fewer workers.

It will have a different topology.
