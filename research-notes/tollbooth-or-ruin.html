Tollbooth or Ruin: Why Some Middles Get Rich and Others Get Hollowed

Some middles become tollbooths. Others become ruins. The difference is whether a critical connective function can capture the value it creates.

Date
2026-07-04
Author
Precision Analytica

Some middles become tollbooths. Others become ruins.

A taxi dispatcher once sat between rider and driver. A travel agent once sat between traveler, airline, hotel, and itinerary. Local brokers, small intermediaries, school counselors, middle managers, mentors, civic moderators, and professional supervisors all occupied the middle in one form or another. They connected endpoints. They reduced search costs. They translated needs. They carried context.

But their fate has not been the same.

Some have been bypassed. The local dispatcher disappears into the ride-hailing platform. The travel agent loses ground to Expedia, Booking, Ctrip, or other online platforms. The small merchant becomes dependent on the marketplace. The local restaurant becomes dependent on the delivery app.

The middle has not vanished. It has been rebuilt as software, payment rail, platform, ranking system, credential gate, or algorithmic access point.

The same connective function that once looked ordinary can become enormously valuable once someone can meter it.

This is why the common complaint that “the middle is being hollowed out” is both true and incomplete. Some of the most powerful firms in the world are middle positions: payment networks, app stores, marketplaces, search engines, cloud platforms, delivery platforms, booking platforms.1 They sit between endpoints and charge for the passage.

So the real question is sharper: why do some middles become tollbooths while others become ruins?

The answer is not whether they connect. It is whether the connective function is both critical and capturable. If a middle position performs a function the system depends on, and if it can capture value from that function, it becomes powerful. If it performs a critical function but cannot capture the value it creates, it becomes a load-bearing wall: necessary, underpriced, and easy to damage because the value it provides becomes visible only after the wall has cracked.

This distinction matters far beyond business strategy. It helps explain the anxiety of the middle class, the weakness of the political center, the vulnerability of middle management, and the danger AI poses to the human formation layer beneath expertise.

The Fork in the Middle

A middle position is not a moral category. It is a functional one.

A middle connects endpoints. It may connect buyer and seller, student and standard, taxpayer and public good, junior worker and expert judgment, voter and institution, patient and doctor, local knowledge and organizational strategy. But middle positions have different fates depending on two variables.

The first is criticality. How much does the system depend on the function? Payment settlement is critical. Monetary stability is critical. Public education is critical. Training future professionals is critical. Middle-class family formation is critical. Institutional trust is critical. Strategy-to-execution translation is critical. Some of these functions are glamorous; most are not. But when they fail, the system becomes brittle.

The second is appropriability, the economics term for whether an actor can capture the value it creates.2 In plainer language: can it set up a tollbooth? Can it control access, meter the flow, attach a payment, claim ownership, require participation, or receive a legal charter?

Put the two together and the map becomes clear.

If a function is highly critical and easy to capture, the middle becomes a tollbooth. Visa, app stores, payment rails, and major platforms fit this pattern. They connect endpoints and can charge for passage. The taxi dispatcher to Uber, or the travel agent to online booking platforms, shows the movement: a once human, local, partly relational connector becomes a digital chokepoint where every search, ride, booking, ranking, and payment can be tracked and priced.

If a function is highly critical but hard to capture, the middle becomes a load-bearing wall. Mentors, middle managers, civic institutions, public-order maintainers, apprenticeship tasks, and middle-class households often fit this pattern. They carry the system, but their value appears somewhere else.

If a function has low social contribution but retains a high ability to charge, the middle becomes parasitic. This is the dark path of platform power. A connector may begin as useful infrastructure, then preserve its toll through lock-in, switching costs, network effects, or regulatory capture after its contribution to the flow has declined.

If a function is low in both criticality and capture capacity, it is correctly marginal. Not every middle deserves rescue. Some intermediaries disappear because they no longer add enough value.

The important cell is the second one: high criticality, low capture. That is where the modern center gets hollowed out.

The Middle Class as the Load-Bearing Wall

The middle class is often described as “squeezed.” The word is useful, but too passive. It makes the middle class sound like an income band caught between rich and poor. That misses its deeper function.

The middle class is a reproduction layer.

It is the layer through which a society tries to turn work into taxes, taxes into public goods, public goods into ordinary life, ordinary life into family formation, family formation into education, and education into the next generation of competence. It is where private effort and public capacity meet.

The middle class depends on institutions, but it also sustains them. It uses schools, hospitals, insurance systems, roads, mortgages, professional credentials, local services, public safety, and retirement systems. It pays into the tax base. It complies with rules. It stabilizes neighborhoods. It sends children into the education system. It carries much of the expectation that normal life should still be possible through work, discipline, and delayed gratification.

Its function is highly critical.

But it is hard to capture.

The middle class cannot charge for the stability it provides. Its value appears as a functioning society, not as a revenue stream. Its contribution shows up in public-school demand, neighborhood continuity, ordinary compliance, institutional trust, and intergenerational aspiration. These are real outputs, but they do not belong cleanly to the middle class as a claimant.

This is why the middle class cannot be reduced to the question of whether it pays the highest tax rate or receives the fewest benefits in some narrow technical sense. The more important issue is net load: taxes, transfers, housing costs, education costs, healthcare exposure, retirement risk, public-service dependence, family formation pressure, and limited exit capacity taken together.

The rich have exit.3 They can privatize schools, buy security, relocate, hire around institutional failure, optimize taxes, purchase access, or move assets.

The poor have voice, at least in principle. Hardship is visible. Protection claims are morally legible. Political systems can name poverty more easily than they can name the load-bearing role of the middle.

The middle has attachment. It remains tied to mortgages, schools, occupations, neighborhoods, career ladders, public order, and the promise that the system still rewards ordinary effort. Attachment makes the middle responsible, but it also makes it vulnerable.

This is where a public-choice explanation is not enough.4 Yes, the middle class is diffuse, and it often fails to organize as a single claimant. But the deeper problem is that its value is hard to capture. In some cases, no meter can exist. In others, a claim could be built, but no organized claimant forms.

That distinction matters.

For formation and tacit knowledge, the problem is often measurement. You cannot charge cleanly for “the junior became wise” or “the child absorbed institutional trust.” The value is real but not easily metered.

For the middle class and the political center, the problem is often organization. A claim could be made around net load, family formation, public dependence, and intergenerational reproduction, but the group is internally divided. It is aspirational upward, anxious downward, professionally fragmented, geographically split, and culturally cross-pressured. It does not speak with one voice because it does not live as one class in a simple political sense.

So low capture has two sources. Sometimes the meter cannot be built. Sometimes the claimant has not been manufactured.

That is why the middle class can be critical and weak at the same time. It is not powerless because it has nothing. It is vulnerable because it has too much to lose and too few clean ways to exit.

A Brief Word on the Political Center

The political center is a familiar version of the same problem, though it should not carry the whole theory.

Moderate voters may exist in large numbers. Many people hold mixed views, dislike conflict, prefer competence, and want institutions to function. But the political poles mobilize more easily because they provide identity, enemy, grievance, and action. The center offers compromise, restraint, and continuity. These are important, but they do not carry the same mobilizing force.

The center’s success often looks like nothing happened: no breakdown, no crisis, no institutional failure. That makes its value hard to claim.

This is why the political center can have mass without becoming a political actor. It lowers conflict, but it does not own the victory. It sustains the room in which others fight, but no one pays for the room.

AI and the Stranded Formation Layer

AI makes the theory sharper because it exposes the difference between producing work and reproducing the worker.

Many knowledge-work tasks are double-use tasks. They produce an output, but they also form the person doing the work.

A junior analyst writes a first draft. The visible product is the draft. But the hidden product is the analyst learning how to structure an argument, check a source, notice an inconsistency, absorb a senior reviewer’s standard, and develop judgment.

A junior lawyer prepares a memo. The visible product is legal text. The hidden product is professional formation.

A student writes an essay. The visible product is the essay. The hidden product is the mind being trained.

A middle manager reviews work.5 The visible product is quality control. The hidden product is the transmission of standards. The middle manager is not only an administrative layer; she is often the connector through which strategy becomes practice and junior effort becomes institutional memory.

AI can capture the visible product. It can generate the draft, summarize the source, produce the memo, polish the slide, write the code, or assemble the report. The vendor can meter prompts, tokens, subscriptions, APIs, enterprise access, and output.

But AI does not meter the human middle. It bypasses it.

The junior analyst is not being priced more accurately. She is being disintermediated. The old production task migrates to a vendor-owned chokepoint, while the formation value that used to ride along with that task is stranded.

That is the crucial point: formation was a joint product of production. It was never priced separately because it did not need to be. The organization got the work and trained the worker at the same time. Once AI separates those two functions, the visible output remains while the invisible formation channel may weaken.

This is not a prediction of inevitable collapse. AI may also create new training pathways. Better tools can make juniors learn faster, expose them to more examples, and let seniors supervise in new ways. But that does not happen automatically. The framework surfaces a risk: if firms automate junior work without rebuilding apprenticeship, they may preserve short-term output while weakening the pipeline of future judgment.

The institution can look more efficient and become less capable of reproducing the people who made it efficient.

This is the hidden capability erosion problem.

The same logic applies beyond firms.6 Schools can preserve completed assignments while weakening learning. Professions can preserve deliverables while weakening standards. Organizations can preserve documents while weakening memory. The public sees output. The system may lose formation.

Formation is the ultimate high-criticality, low-capture function.7 Everyone benefits from competent people, but no one easily owns the moment in which competence is formed.

Repricing Requires an Agent: A Meter or a Claimant

It is tempting to say: recognize the middle, value the middle, recharge the center. But that is too soft.

The center cannot become another pole without ceasing to be the center. If it builds a hard identity, chooses an enemy, and organizes around a purified claim, it becomes one more pole in the field.

The better answer is not to recharge the center. It is to reprice the load.

But repricing does not happen by wishing. It requires an agent. The type of agent depends on the source of low capture.

The first source is measurement failure. The value is critical but cannot be metered by ordinary transactions. Formation is the clearest case. The work produces competence, standards, judgment, and tacit knowledge, but these outputs do not arrive as clean invoices.

This is why scarcity alone does not solve the problem. If the producer of formation cannot internalize the value, the market can underprice it indefinitely. Competent people may become scarce, but the training moments that created them still do not send a bill. Output will be counted. Training will be assumed. The future expert will become an externality.

Here the remedy is a manufactured meter: a charter, levy, mandate, credential, public funding rule, or institutional accounting system that forces the value into view.

A private platform manufactures a chokepoint for profit. A public-purpose charter manufactures a chokepoint for a function the market would underprice. The point is not that every charter is good. Licensing can protect standards or protect incumbents. Credentialing can preserve quality or create exclusion. Public institutions can maintain capacity or become rigid. But the category is necessary.

Training levies, apprenticeship systems, professional chambers, public education funding mandates, and supervised credential pathways are attempts, imperfect and contestable, to manufacture a meter around formation value that firms and markets would otherwise treat as someone else’s problem.8

The second source is organization failure. The value is not impossible to describe, but no claimant exists to own it. This is closer to the middle class and the political center.

Here the remedy is not only a meter. It is a manufactured claimant.

A middle class organized around gross income will always fracture. A middle class organized around ideology will be absorbed into the poles. But a middle class organized around net load, family formation, institutional dependence, and intergenerational reproduction might become a clearer constituency.

That does not mean inventing a new class war. It means naming the function. The middle class is not just an income band. It is the social layer through which ordinary life is reproduced. If that function matters, it needs language, representation, and accounting instruments that make the load visible before it becomes collapse.

The political center faces a similar problem. It cannot mobilize by becoming louder than the poles. But it can be represented through institutions that reward competence, coalition-building, local public goods, procedural stability, and cross-pressured voters. The remedy is not emotional charge for its own sake. It is organizational form.

So the prescription has two limbs.

When the problem is measurement failure, manufacture a meter.

When the problem is organization failure, manufacture a claimant.

Both are ways of making high-criticality, low-capture value visible enough to defend.

What Should Be Protected?

The framework does not say that every middle layer should be preserved.

That would be nostalgia, not theory.

Some intermediaries disappear because their function is no longer critical. Some should disappear because they were never useful enough. Some platforms deserve their toll because they improve the flow. Some platforms become parasitic because lock-in, switching costs, network effects, or regulation preserve the toll after the contribution declines.

The real question is narrower:

Which high-criticality, low-capture functions must be protected because the system depends on them but markets, organizations, or politics will not price them correctly?

This question changes how we think about the middle class. It should not be understood only as an income band. It should be understood as a net-load position and reproduction layer. Policy should ask how taxes, transfers, housing, education, healthcare, retirement, local order, and family formation combine to determine whether ordinary competence can reproduce itself.

It changes how we think about AI. We should not ask only which jobs will be automated. We should ask which formation channels will be stranded.

It changes how we think about institutions. Mature systems need maintenance, translation, training, compliance, trust, and judgment reproduction. These functions often look boring from the edge, but they are what keep the system from becoming brittle.

And it changes how we think about platforms. The opposite of a hollowed middle is not a healthy society. Sometimes it is a tollbooth. Sometimes it is a parasite. A society that destroys all unmetered middle functions may not become more efficient. It may transfer connective power to actors who can charge for what everyone else still needs.

Conclusion: The Wall Cracks Late

The middle is easy to misunderstand because its best work often looks like normal life.

A good mentor leaves behind a competent person, not a receipt. A healthy middle class leaves behind functioning institutions, stable neighborhoods, and children who still believe effort can become a future. A good political center leaves behind lower conflict, not a trophy. A good apprenticeship leaves behind judgment, not just output.

These are critical functions, but they do not all become tollbooths.

That is why mature systems are tempted to optimize them away. They cut the middle manager, bypass the junior task, squeeze the middle-class household, ignore the civic moderator, automate the draft, and celebrate the platform that can charge for the flow.

For a while, nothing seems broken.

That is the danger of the load-bearing middle. Its value is useful in a way no one can appropriate until the wall has already cracked.

Some middles become tollbooths. Others become ruins.

The task of a mature society is to know which walls must never be mistaken for waste.
