Theme

Artificial Intelligence, Institutions, and Human Capability

Unbundling Formation from Performance: An Institutional Theory of Artificial Intelligence and Human Capability

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: July 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Debates over artificial intelligence and work are usually organized around tasks: which tasks machines can perform, and what happens to the workers who performed them. This paper develops a theory of AI-driven institutional unbundling: the separation of visible performance from the hidden formation that historically rode on performing the work. Many institutions bundled output and formation because separating them was costly. Generative AI lowers that separation cost. It can supply performance while bypassing the human process through which capability, judgment, attachment, and responsibility were formed. I locate the mechanism in a collective-action asymmetry: unbundling is cheap, private, and immediate, while rebundling formation is costly, collective, and delayed. A simple model shows why the harm is uneven. Unbundling is more likely when immediate gain and maintenance cost are high and the deciding actor's internalized horizon is low; conditional on unbundling, social formation loss rises with bundle strength. The highest-risk cases lie near the preservation threshold, where formative value is large enough to matter but not sufficiently internalized to stop unbundling. The contribution is a positive theory of where AI hollows institutions, where it does not, and when formation can be rebuilt.

Keywords: artificial intelligence; institutions; human capital formation; tacit knowledge; collective action; automation; apprenticeship

Delegation Risk Homeostasis: Why More Capable AI Agents Need Not Produce Fewer Incidents

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: June 11, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Frontier AI models are becoming more capable, but enterprise incidents from AI agents need not decline. This paper explains why. When firms delegate tasks to AI agents, capability improvements reduce the failure risk of fixed tasks, but governance-constrained firms expand delegated authority until their incident-absorption capacity binds again. The result is delegation risk homeostasis: incident intensity per unit of agent-task exposure can remain stable even as models improve. The model also predicts severity drift, because constant-frequency incidents migrate into higher-authority tasks, and release-cycle overshoot, because firms may expand authority faster than field performance is learned. The framework identifies the underwriting objects for AI agent liability insurance: governance capacity, exposure volume, delegated authority, control quality, release-window risk, and claim-layer containment. The implication is not that model quality is irrelevant, but that model generation alone is not a sufficient rating variable.

Keywords: AI agents; delegation; risk compensation; operational risk; insurance underwriting

The Second Displacement: AI, Knowledge Work, and the Collapse of the Professional Bargain

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: June 6, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Artificial intelligence may unsettle educated work before it produces visible mass unemployment. This paper argues that the first modern labor displacement weakened the bread-and-meaning bargain of industrial and special-skill labor, while AI now puts pressure on a second bargain: the professional status once attached to higher education, symbolic production, and trained judgment. The central claim is that AI can generate a meaning shock before an income shock. By lowering the scarcity value of first-pass symbolic output, improving employers' outside options, and compressing junior career ladders, AI can weaken the social meaning of professional work even where employment remains intact, especially if institutions automate junior work faster than they redesign apprenticeship. The paper develops a bottleneck account of personal satisfaction, three propositions, an occupational taxonomy, and a theory of discursive overheating. It also clarifies the institutional conditions under which a successor bargain might preserve judgment formation without becoming a rescue program for the already credentialed.

Keywords: artificial intelligence; knowledge work; symbolic labor; professional identity; automation; judgment; apprenticeship; credentialing; recognition; platform politics; labor displacement

When Bread Rebinds: A Leontief Theory of Symbolic Mandate Exhaustion

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: June 9, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: I develop a theory of symbolic mandate exhaustion. Voters require both material security, or bread, and social meaning, and the two are poor substitutes near important social baselines, so welfare is governed by the lower of the two. When meaning is the initially binding constraint, a political movement can raise welfare by supplying recognition, dignity, identity validation, and narrative repair. Credible entry, however, requires a minimum viable symbolic package rather than a finely tuned marginal adjustment: a challenger must differentiate itself enough to be read as a distinct political type. Conditional on entry the movement supplies this package and devotes its remaining capacity to material delivery. When the resulting allocation leaves bread below repaired meaning, entry raises welfare and switches the binding constraint from meaning to bread. Past that switch, further symbolic supply has zero marginal welfare value in the Leontief limit and sharply diminished value under low-substitution preferences. The movement's symbolic mandate is exhausted unless it can convert recognition into reconstruction. A reference-dependent extension shows when exhaustion of the instrument becomes outright decay of support. Brief illustrations distinguish recognition movements that exhaust once they govern from those whose mandate persists because the material delivery test has not arrived.

Keywords: symbolic politics; populism; recognition; redistribution; identity; complementarity

Bread and Dignity: The Dual Engine of Western Liberal Society

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: May 30, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: This paper develops a conceptual framework for why the tension between capitalism and progressive reform was historically productive in Western liberal society and why it now appears to be overheating. Capitalism organizes material survival through markets, competition, innovation, and surplus generation; progressive, liberal, and civic institutions process the public legitimacy of that order through rights, protection, inclusion, recognition, and reform. The duality works when moral critique becomes durable repair. It breaks down when material repair weakens, civil-society mediation thins, and recognition is asked to compensate for insecurities it cannot solve. Platform moralism is treated not as the original cause but as an accelerant that turns unresolved insecurity into visible moral conflict. The paper emphasizes three mediating structures often smoothed over by the bread/meaning framework: race, civil society and religion, and the state as both repair mechanism and source of insecurity. The argument points toward repair social democracy: a politics of bread plus dignity.

Keywords: Bread and Dignity; capitalism; progressivism; Polanyi; social protection; recognition politics; civil society; platform moralism; institutional legitimacy; repair social democracy

Social Structure, Politics, and Institutional Lag

The Dislocated Middle: Stranded Dignity and Institutional Lag in Bread-and-Meaning Life Paths

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: July 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: This paper develops a theory of middle hollowing in growth societies. The productive middle is defined as a position in an institutional arrangement: households with sunk commitment to licensed life paths that bundle material viability, or Bread, with socially recognized Meaning. A fast-moving payoff frontier can reduce the Bread return of these paths before institutions and moral codes can relicense new paths carrying public dignity. The result is stranded dignity: the old route remains honorable but no longer secures material life. The paper formalizes this dual-speed dislocation, identifies the stability-tipping boundary of recognition dynamics, characterizes the sign condition for the frontier-velocity comparative static of the stranded segment, and shows how repeated shocks generate a persistent Bread-Meaning wedge under a two-rate exposure condition. It uses artificial intelligence as a contemporary acceleration case in which labor-market dynamics can move faster than family-supporting institutions. It distinguishes the mechanism from labor-market polarization, inequality, cultural lag, and political center erosion.

Keywords: middle class; institutional change; cultural lag; moral code; Bread and Meaning; social meaning; stranded dignity; technological change; political economy

The Erosion of Visible Moderation: Endogenous Salience Tilting and Political Polarization

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: April 21, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: The erosion of visible moderation can arise without rapid belief change. Citizens hold latent beliefs across multiple political dimensions. Social-media engagement reveals and amplifies dimensions where disagreement is greatest, tilting the public projection weights that map latent beliefs into a visible political axis. A mean-field click equilibrium with active-passive attention yields a projection-rotation result: when active attention is ordered toward high-conflict dimensions, visible political variance expands. Under a visible-tail regularity condition, this variance expansion appears as a thinner moderate center and larger extreme share. A two-block case shows the mechanism transparently: high-conflict issues receive positive projection wedges, low-conflict issues receive negative wedges, and the visible axis rotates away from the mainstream-media benchmark. The electoral implication is that campaigns compete not only to persuade voters, but also to locate voters on a changing public political axis.

Keywords: political polarization; erosion of visible moderation; center hollowing; projection rotation; social media; mean-field equilibrium

Education and Political Polarization: A Level or Slope Effect?

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: April 3, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: We ask whether education changes the rate at which partisan identity hardens or only the baseline propensity to switch. We develop a mean-field model of partisan sorting in which agents balance tribal conformity against private signals. In the model, education reduces susceptibility to tribal pull but does not affect the hardening rate itself. The implication is a level effect, not a slope effect: education shifts baseline switching rates without changing the tenure profile of partisan lock-in. We test this prediction using ANES panels from 1972-1976 and 2016-2024. In both eras, the education-by-tenure interaction is close to zero and statistically insignificant. College graduates are less likely to switch party identification at baseline, but the Converse curve is essentially parallel across education groups. The evidence is consistent with a separation between media susceptibility and identity formation, and it does not support models in which education speeds convergence to stable partisanship.

Keywords: partisan sorting; political polarization; Converse curve; party identification; identity formation; education

Technology Diffusion, Markets, and Asset Pricing

Carrier Reproduction and the Transmission of Technological Innovation

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: July 1, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Technological innovations often spread faster than the capacity to reproduce them locally. A hospital may perform robotic procedures, a factory may run CNC equipment, or a firm may deploy AI tools while failing to renew the people who can operate, diagnose, repair, teach, and adapt the underlying capability. This article develops a framework and research agenda centered on carrier reproduction. An individual carrier is a person with independent command of a technological task who can help reproduce that command in others; organizations and ecosystems are carrier-bearing systems insofar as they renew the required mix of such individuals. The framework distinguishes structural transmission - the match among a design's reproduction requirements, local social transmission fabric, and the available carrier base - from reproductive transmission, the process by which local operation renews carriers over time. Reproductive decoupling occurs when adoption or output continues while carrier reproduction weakens; productive decoupling is the self-concealing case in which real output masks that weakening. The paper specifies diagnostic indicators, falsification conditions, validation designs, and illustrative settings including robotic surgery and AI-assisted knowledge work.

Keywords: technological innovation; technology transmission; carrier reproduction; local social transmission fabric; reproductive coupling; reproductive decoupling; productive decoupling; technology transfer; learning-by-doing; absorptive capacity; AI

Diagnosing Adoption Dynamics: A Structural Approach to Diffusion Modeling

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: January 1, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Why do identical product launches succeed rapidly in some markets while languishing in others? Managers facing sluggish adoption confront a diagnostic challenge: does the problem reflect population composition or weak signaling? Standard diffusion models cannot answer this question because they lack micro-foundations connecting who adopts to how adoption unfolds. We develop a coordination game framework where heterogeneous consumers balance social proof against aspirational imitation. The equilibrium yields a reduced-form specification with structurally interpretable coefficients: persistence reflects mass-market conformity pressure; second-order dynamics capture delayed follower response to innovator behavior; shock responsiveness depends on early adopter share and behavioral heterogeneity. This structural mapping enables root-cause diagnosis from adoption data alone and maps these diagnostics to actionable levers: whether to invest in early-adopter targeting and influencer partnerships or improve visibility and social proof. We validate parameter recovery through Monte Carlo simulation and demonstrate applications spanning linguistic innovation to online discourse, contexts differing by five orders of magnitude in timescale yet conforming to the same specification. The framework equips practitioners to diagnose diffusion problems and design appropriately targeted interventions.

Keywords: adoption diagnostics; market heterogeneity; coordination games; Bass model; consumer behavior; diffusion model

Platform Competition Through Composition: Engagement Heterogeneity, Data Accumulation, and Market Tipping

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: December 29, 2025; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: Why do smaller platforms sometimes overtake larger rivals? This paper studies one mechanism through which user composition can matter as much as user count. Conditional on an early sorting advantage among high-engagement producers, platforms serving those users accumulate data faster, improve quality sooner, and may cross a tipping threshold that leads to dominance. Three results follow. First, the dominance condition links design quality and the engagement premium to the tipping threshold: conditional on Phase 0 producer sorting, the platform whose design-composition product exceeds its rival's wins from any equal or better starting position. Second, greater engagement heterogeneity lowers the tipping threshold and implies a cross-market association between contribution inequality and concentration. Third, concentration has distributional welfare effects: producers benefit from quality improvements while consumers bear mismatch costs. The model therefore suggests caution with uniform policy thresholds and points toward engagement-contingent interventions as a possible design principle.

Keywords: platform competition; data accumulation; market concentration; user heterogeneity; privacy regulation; tipping dynamics

Horizon Sorting: Investor Capacity and the Shape of Price-pressure Correction

Author: Arthur Claudino; Date: June 5, 2026; Status: Working Paper

Abstract: This paper develops a rational-expectations model of horizon sorting in asset markets. A single asset is exposed to flow-pressure shocks of different persistence. Investor classes differ in trading frictions, risk penalties, and shared capacity. Market clearing makes correction paths depend on one flow-persistence mode and common internal redistribution modes. Shared class capacity turns dynamic trading-friction nesting into horizon sorting: fast capital allocates relatively more capacity to fast-decaying shocks unless slow capital's risk-bearing advantage is sufficiently large. Stylized numerical exercises illustrate inventory migration, normalized sorting strength, and transient-flow recovery restrictions. The model predicts correction-path shape, not only scalar demand elasticity.

Keywords: horizon sorting; investor capacity; price pressure; demand elasticity; slow-moving capital; institutional investors; transaction costs
